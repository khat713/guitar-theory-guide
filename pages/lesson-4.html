MODULE 4
MAJOR AND MINOR SCALES

A scale is a pattern of fret jumps. Learn the major and minor formulas and you can play in any key, starting from any note on the guitar.

What Is a Scale?

A scale is a pattern of fret jumps starting from one note. Think of it like following directions: "jump 2, jump 2, jump 1, jump 2..." and you land on the right notes every time.

Scales matter because every song lives in a key, and every key has its scale. Know the scale, and you know which notes to play for melodies and solos.

💡
You only need to memorize one formula per scale type. Then apply it starting from any note. One formula unlocks every key!

Two Building Blocks: W and H

Every scale formula uses just two moves: W (Whole step) and H (Half step).

H: Half Step Move 1 fret. The tiniest jump. E to F is an H. B to C is an H.
W: Whole Step Move 2 frets. Two tiny jumps combined. C to D is a W. A to B is a W.

Read the formula left to right. Jump that many frets each time to find the next note.

The Major Scale Formula

The major scale sounds bright and happy, think "Do Re Mi." There is just one formula, and it works starting from any note:

W  –  W  –  H  –  W  –  W  –  W  –  H Root → +2 → +2 → +1 → +2 → +2 → +2 → +1 = octave

That gives you 7 jumps and 8 notes total (the last note is the same as the first, just higher up).

Building C Major Step by Step

Start on C and follow the formula. Each row is one jump.

C major is all white keys. No sharps, no flats. That's why music theory always starts here; it's the cleanest example to see how the formula works.

C Major on Guitar

Play C major on one string, starting at fret 8 on the low E. Follow the W–W–H–W–W–W–H jumps. 

Low E:  8   10   12   13   15   17   19   20
Note:   C    D    E    F    G    A    B    C
        |    W    W    H    W    W    W    H  |

C major scale on the low E string, fret 8 to fret 20, one octave

Play it slowly. Hear that "Do Re Mi" sound? That's the major scale, happy and bright! The tiny H jumps (frets 12→13 and 19→20) are what give it that cheerful feeling.

💡
Moveable shapes. Shift this whole pattern up 2 frets and you're playing D major. The shape is the same; only the starting fret changes. That's the power of learning formulas!

The Minor Scale Formula

The minor scale sounds darker and sadder; think moody, emotional songs. It uses the same W and H jumps as major, just in a different order:

W  –  H  –  W  –  W  –  H  –  W  –  W Root → +2 → +1 → +2 → +2 → +1 → +2 → +2 = octave

Notice the H comes earlier than in the major formula. That small change is what makes the scale sound sad instead of happy!

Building A Minor Step by Step

Start on A and follow the minor formula. Each row is one jump.

AStart here: this is the root note
W → BJump 2 frets up from A
H → CJump 1 fret up from B (B to C is always 1 fret)
W → DJump 2 frets up from C
W → EJump 2 frets up from D
H → FJump 1 fret up from E (E to F is always 1 fret)
W → GJump 2 frets up from F
W → AJump 2 frets up from G, back to A!

A Minor on Guitar

A minor is great to start with because it begins on the open A string, no need to move up the neck at all! 

A str:  0   2   3   5   7   8   10   12
Note:   A   B   C   D   E   F   G    A
        |   W   H   W   W   H   W    W  |

A minor scale on the A string, from open to fret 12, one octave

Play this right after the C major run. Hear how different it feels: darker, sadder, like it hasn't quite found home yet. Same kind of run, totally different mood!

The Relative Major/Minor Relationship

Look at the two scales you just built:

C majorC   D   E   F   G   A   B
A minorA   B   C   D   E   F   G

They use the exact same seven notes! The only difference is where you start. C major starts on C; A minor starts on A.

This is called the relative minor. Every major scale has a sad twin hiding inside it, starting on the 6th note.

⚠
Same notes, different feel. Start and end on C: sounds happy (major). Start and end on A: sounds sad (minor). The notes haven't changed; just where you call "home."

Why This Matters in Practice

When someone says a song is "in G major," it means: the notes of the G major scale are the notes that sound good in that song. Melodies, chords, solos: they all come from the same scale.

Learn the C major shape at fret 8, then slide the whole thing to fret 10 and you're in D major. The shape never changes; just your hand position!

💡
This is how guitarists solo. They find the scale that fits the song, then explore those notes. The scale is your map; everything else is how you travel through it.

Practice

Exercise 1: Build G Major from the Formula

Use the formula W–W–H–W–W–W–H. Start on G and make each jump. Try to figure it out before peeking!

Major formula: W – W – H – W – W – W – H
Starting note: G

W = 2 frets, H = 1 fret. Use this as your roadmap: G – G# – A – A# – B – C – C# – D – D# – E – F – F# – G
G major, step by step:
  • G: start here
  • W → A (jump 2)
  • W → B (jump 2)
  • H → C (jump 1)
  • W → D (jump 2)
  • W → E (jump 2)
  • W → F# (jump 2; needs a sharp to keep the pattern right!)
  • H → G (jump 1)
Result: G   A   B   C   D   E   F#   G

G major has one sharp: F#. Whenever you start the major formula from a note other than C, you'll usually get at least one sharp or flat.

Exercise 2: Find the Relative Minor of G Major

The relative minor starts on the 6th note of any major scale. Count up through G major and find it!

G major notes:
1=G   2=A   3=B   4=C   5=D   6=?   7=F#

What is note number 6? That's the start of the relative minor.
The 6th note of G major is E.

E minor is the relative minor of G major. They share all the same notes, just starting in a different place:

E minor: E   F#   G   A   B   C   D   E

Check it with the minor formula W–H–W–W–H–W–W:
  • E → F# (W)
  • F# → G (H)
  • G → A (W)
  • A → B (W)
  • B → C (H)
  • C → D (W)
  • D → E (W)
Same notes as G major! A happy song in G major and a sad song in E minor can use the exact same notes; it all depends on where you "come home" to.
