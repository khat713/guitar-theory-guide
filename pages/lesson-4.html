MODULE 4
MAJOR AND MINOR SCALES

A scale is a pattern of fret jumps. Learn the major and minor formulas and you can play in any key, starting from any note on the guitar.

What Is a Scale?

A scale is a pattern of fret jumps starting from one note. Think of it like following directions: "jump 2, jump 2, jump 1, jump 2..." and you land on the right notes every time.

Scales matter because every song lives in a key, and every key has its scale. Know the scale, and you know which notes to play for melodies and solos.

💡
You only need to memorize one formula per scale type. Then apply it starting from any note. One formula unlocks every key!

Two Building Blocks: W and H

Every scale formula uses just two moves: W (Whole step) and H (Half step).

H: Half Step Move 1 fret. The tiniest jump. E to F is an H. B to C is an H.
W: Whole Step Move 2 frets. Two tiny jumps combined. C to D is a W. A to B is a W.

Read the formula left to right. Jump that many frets each time to find the next note.

The Major Scale Formula

The major scale sounds bright and happy — think "Do Re Mi." There is just one formula, and it works starting from any note:

W  –  W  –  H  –  W  –  W  –  W  –  H Root → +2 → +2 → +1 → +2 → +2 → +2 → +1 = octave

That gives you 7 jumps and 8 notes total (the last note is the same as the first, just higher up).

✓
C major is all white keys. No sharps, no flats. That's why music theory always starts here — it's the cleanest example to see how the formula works.

C Major on Guitar

You can play the C major scale as a single-string run starting from C on fret 8 of the low E string. The fret numbers follow directly from the interval formula. 

Low E:  8   10   12   13   15   17   19   20
Note:   C    D    E    F    G    A    B    C
        |    W    W    H    W    W    W    H  |

C major scale on the low E string, fret 8 to fret 20, one octave

Play it slowly. Listen for the familiar "Do Re Mi" sound, which is the major scale. The H gaps (frets 12→13 and 19→20) are the narrow steps that give the major scale its characteristic lift.

💡
Moveable shapes. This exact fret-distance pattern works starting from any note on any string. Shift the whole run up 2 frets and you have D major. That's why scale formulas are so powerful on guitar.

The Natural Minor Scale Formula

The natural minor scale has a darker, more serious sound. It's the foundation for minor keys, blues-influenced rock, and most "sad" or "emotional" melodies. The formula rearranges the same building blocks as major, but in a different order with a different mood:

W  –  H  –  W  –  W  –  H  –  W  –  W Root → +2 → +1 → +2 → +2 → +1 → +2 → +2 = octave

Notice the H steps have shifted positions. The critical change: there's an H on step 2 instead of step 3, which pulls the third note down a half step. That lowered third is what creates the minor sound.

W → G2 frets up from F lands on G

A Minor on Guitar

A minor is particularly convenient to play because it starts on the open A string (fret 0). You don't need to shift up the neck at all. 

A str:  0   2   3   5   7   8   10   12
Note:   A   B   C   D   E   F   G    A
        |   W   H   W   W   H   W    W  |

A minor scale on the A string, from open to fret 12, one octave

Play this back to back with the C major run. Listen carefully. You'll hear a different emotional color: lower, darker, and unresolved, even though both are simple ascending runs.

The Relative Major/Minor Relationship

Compare the two scales you just built:

C majorC   D   E   F   G   A   B
A minorA   B   C   D   E   F   G

They contain exactly the same seven notes. The only difference is where you start. C major starts on C; A minor starts on A, which is the sixth note of the C major scale.

This is called the relative minor relationship. Every major scale has a relative minor that shares its notes, starting on scale degree 6. Every minor scale has a relative major starting on scale degree 3.

⚠
Same notes, different feel. The mood shift, whether major-bright or minor-dark, comes entirely from which note you treat as home. Start and end on C: major. Start and end on A: minor. The notes haven't changed.

Why This Matters in Practice

When someone says a song is "in the key of G major," they mean: the notes of the G major scale are the palette for that song. Melodies use those notes. Chords are built from those notes. When you play lead guitar, those are the notes that sound right.

When a guitarist learns a scale pattern on the fretboard, they're learning the shape of a scale: a fingering pattern that can be moved up or down the neck to any key. Learn the C major shape starting at fret 8, then shift it to fret 10 and you're playing D major. The shape is the same; the key changes with position.

💡
Tabs and lead playing. When you see a guitarist noodle over a chord progression, they're navigating a scale pattern. The scale defines which notes are available. Everything else is phrasing and feel.

Practice

Exercise 1: Build G Major from the Formula

Using the major scale formula W–W–H–W–W–W–H, build the G major scale starting from G. Write out each step before revealing the answer.

Major formula: W – W – H – W – W – W – H
Starting note: G

Apply each interval in order. Remember: W = 2 frets, H = 1 fret through the chromatic sequence: G – G# – A – A# – B – C – C# – D – D# – E – F – F# – G
G major scale, step by step:
  • G: root
  • W → A (2 frets up: G → G# → A)
  • W → B (2 frets up: A → A# → B)
  • H → C (1 fret up: B → C)
  • W → D (2 frets up: C → C# → D)
  • W → E (2 frets up: D → D# → E)
  • W → F# (2 frets up: E → F → F#); note that the sharp is required to keep the pattern consistent
  • H → G (1 fret up: F# → G)
Result: G   A   B   C   D   E   F#   G

G major has one sharp: F#. Every time you apply the major formula starting from a note other than C, you'll end up with at least one sharp or flat to keep the pattern consistent.

Exercise 2: Find the Relative Minor of G Major

The relative minor starts on the 6th degree of the major scale. Count up through G major to find it, then write out the resulting minor scale.

G major degrees:
1=G   2=A   3=B   4=C   5=D   6=?   7=F#

What is the 6th degree? What scale does it produce?
The 6th degree of G major is E.

E minor is the relative minor of G major. They share the same seven notes, just starting from a different place:

E minor: E   F#   G   A   B   C   D   E

Verify with the minor formula W–H–W–W–H–W–W:
  • E: root
  • W → F# (2 frets up)
  • H → G (1 fret up)
  • W → A (2 frets up)
  • W → B (2 frets up)
  • H → C (1 fret up)
  • W → D (2 frets up)
  • W → E (2 frets up)
Identical notes to G major. Songs that sound major (resolved, bright) and songs that sound minor (dark, emotional) can share the same underlying note set; the difference is where the musical gravity points.
