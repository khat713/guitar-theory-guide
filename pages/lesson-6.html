MODULE 6
CHORD PROGRESSIONS

Songs are made by playing chords one after another. Learn the patterns and you'll recognize them in thousands of songs!

What Is a Chord Progression?

A chord progression is a sequence of chords played one after another. Every song you've ever heard is built on one!

The reason some chords sound great together is simple: they all come from the same scale, like members of the same team.

💡
When all your chords come from the same scale, they always sound good together. That's the big idea behind chord progressions!

Why Certain Chords Belong Together

Chords that all come from the same key are called diatonic chords; "diatonic" just means "from the key."

Think of a key like a friend group. Diatonic chords are all in the same group; they know each other and get along great. A chord from outside the key is like a stranger showing up: it can be dramatic, but it stands out!

💡
Stick to diatonic chords and everything will sound good together automatically. It's like a cheat code!

Building Diatonic Chords

Here's how it works: take any major scale and build a chord on each note using only notes from that same scale.

Because of how the scale is built, some chords come out major (happy) and some come out minor (sad). The scale decides for you! 

C Major Scale:   C    D    E    F    G    A    B
Degree:          1    2    3    4    5    6    7

Build triads using only scale notes (every other note):
  Degree 1:  C - E - G   = C major  (I)
  Degree 2:  D - F - A   = D minor  (ii)
  Degree 3:  E - G - B   = E minor  (iii)
  Degree 4:  F - A - C   = F major  (IV)
  Degree 5:  G - B - D   = G major  (V)
  Degree 6:  A - C - E   = A minor  (vi)
  Degree 7:  B - D - F   = B dim    (vii°)

The seven diatonic chords of C major, built using only notes from the C major scale

The pattern (major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished) is the same in every major key. Learn it once, use it everywhere!

Diatonic chord quality pattern (every major key) I   ii   iii   IV   V   vi   vii° Major Minor Minor Major Major Minor Diminished

The Roman Numeral System

Musicians use Roman numerals to label chords. This is awesome because the same numbers work in any key, like a recipe that works with any ingredients!

Uppercase (I, IV, V)Major chord on that scale degree
Lowercase (ii, iii, vi)Minor chord on that scale degree
° (vii°)Diminished chord on that scale degree
IThe "tonic" [home base chord: where songs feel settled and finished]
VThe "dominant" [the tension chord: it makes you want to go back to I]
IVThe "subdominant" [the warm departure chord: moves you away from home smoothly]
⚠
Case matters. I (uppercase) is a major chord. i (lowercase) would be a minor chord. The case tells you the chord type instantly!

The I–IV–V Progression

This is the most famous chord progression in the world! Blues, country, and rock and roll are almost entirely built on these three chords. 

  Progression:    I       IV      V
  In C major:     C       F       G
  In G major:     G       C       D
  In A major:     A       D       E
  In E major:     E       A       B

The I–IV–V in four common guitar keys, showing the same Roman numerals applied to different root notes

Here's why it feels so good: I is home, IV takes you on a little adventure, and V makes you really want to come back home. That push-pull feeling is what makes music exciting!

✓
Songs using I–IV–V: "Johnny B. Goode," "La Bamba," "Twist and Shout," and virtually every 12-bar blues ever recorded. Learn these three chords and you can jam along to hundreds of songs!

The I–V–vi–IV Progression

This is called the "four-chord song" because a huge number of pop hits use exactly these four chords. You've definitely heard it before, probably hundreds of times! 

  Progression:    I       V       vi      IV
  In C major:     C       G       Am      F
  In G major:     G       D       Em      C
  In D major:     D       A       Bm      G

The I–V–vi–IV in common guitar keys

The vi chord (like Am in C major) is a minor chord, so it gives the song a brief sad moment before bouncing back. That little dip in feeling is what makes this progression so catchy!

The vi–IV–I–V Progression

This uses the exact same four chords as the I–V–vi–IV, but starts on the sad-sounding vi chord. Starting in a different spot changes the whole feeling of the song, like telling the same story but starting at a different chapter! 

  Progression:    vi      IV      I       V
  In C major:     Am      F       C       G
  In G major:     Em      C       G       D

vi–IV–I–V: same chords as the four-chord song, but with a darker feel from starting on minor

This version feels more moody and searching. The chord you start on sets the emotional tone for the whole progression!

The ii–V–I Progression

If I–IV–V is the king of blues and rock, ii–V–I is the king of jazz. It shows up in almost every jazz song ever written! 

  Progression:    ii      V       I
  In C major:     Dm      G       C
  In G major:     Am      D       G
  In F major:     Gm      C       F

The ii–V–I: the cornerstone of jazz harmony

Think of it like a sneeze: ii builds the pressure, V is the "achoo!" moment, and I is the satisfying relief. It's the most complete tension-and-release move in music!

💡
To hear ii–V–I in action, listen to "Fly Me to the Moon" or "Autumn Leaves." Once you know what to listen for, you'll hear it everywhere in jazz!

Tension and Resolution: V Wants to Go to I

The most important move in all of music is going from the V chord back to I. V is the tension chord [it makes you feel unsettled], and I is the resolution chord [it makes everything feel finished and calm].

In C major, the V chord (G) has the note B in it; B is just one tiny step away from C. That closeness makes your ears desperately want to hear C next. When it arrives, it feels like a sigh of relief!

Tonic (I)Home. Stable. Resolved. Tension is released here.
Dominant (V)Maximum tension. Pulls urgently back toward I.
Subdominant (IV)Departure from home. Stable but away from center.
V → IThe most satisfying move in music. Called a "perfect cadence."

Transposing Progressions to Any Key

Here's where Roman numerals really pay off! Once you know a progression as Roman numerals, you can move it to any key super fast.

Just find the diatonic chords for the new key, then apply the same numbers. Done! 

  I–IV–V in different keys:

  Key of C:    C    F    G
  Key of G:    G    C    D
  Key of D:    D    G    A
  Key of A:    A    D    E
  Key of E:    E    A    B

  I–V–vi–IV in different keys:

  Key of C:    C    G    Am   F
  Key of G:    G    D    Em   C
  Key of A:    A    E    F#m  D

The same progressions transposed across keys: Roman numerals stay constant while chord names change

✓
A singer asks you to play in a different key because it suits their voice. If you know the Roman numerals, changing keys takes about ten seconds. You'll look like a pro!

Practice

Exercise 1: I–V–vi–IV in G Major

Write out the I–V–vi–IV chord progression in the key of G major. Work it out from the diatonic chords of G major before revealing the answer.

The key of G major:
Diatonic chords of G major are built on the scale G A B C D E F#.
Apply the I–V–vi–IV pattern. What are the four chords?
Answer: G, D, Em, C
  • I = G major (built on G, the 1st degree)
  • V = D major (built on D, the 5th degree)
  • vi = E minor (built on E, the 6th degree, which is minor because of the diatonic pattern)
  • IV = C major (built on C, the 4th degree)

G, D, Em, and C are four of the most common open chord shapes on guitar. Play them in this order and you'll immediately recognize the sound from dozens of songs!

Exercise 2: Diatonic Chords of A Major

The A major scale is: A B C# D E F# G#. Build all seven diatonic chords using the pattern (Major Minor Minor Major Major Minor Diminished).

A major scale degrees:
1=A   2=B   3=C#   4=D   5=E   6=F#   7=G#
Apply the diatonic quality pattern to each degree.
The seven diatonic chords of A major:
  • I     = A major
  • ii    = B minor
  • iii   = C# minor
  • IV    = D major
  • V     = E major
  • vi    = F# minor
  • vii° = G# diminished

The pattern is identical to C major, just rooted on A instead of C. The quality pattern (Maj Min Min Maj Maj Min Dim) never changes across major keys!
