MODULE 6
CHORD PROGRESSIONS

Why do some chords sound right together while others clash? The answer is diatonic harmony, and once you understand it, you will hear the same patterns in thousands of songs.

What Is a Chord Progression?

A chord progression is a sequence of chords played one after another. That's the entire definition. Every song you have ever heard is built on a chord progression, and the chord changes underneath the melody form the harmonic backbone of the music.

The reason certain progressions appear in thousands of songs is not coincidence. It is because those chords come from the same scale and therefore sound naturally unified. Understanding why they work together is what this lesson is about.

💡
A chord progression is not random. The chords in any good progression share a common origin: the same scale. This is the key insight that unlocks harmonic theory.

Why Certain Chords Belong Together

When chords come from the same key (meaning every note in every chord belongs to the same parent scale) they sound natural together. These are called diatonic chords. "Diatonic" just means "belonging to the key."

Think of a key as a family. All the diatonic chords are members of the same family. They share notes, they recognize each other, and they move between one another smoothly. A chord from outside the key sounds like a stranger showing up uninvited. It can work for dramatic effect, but it requires intention.

💡
Diatonic means "from the key." A diatonic chord is built entirely from notes that exist in the scale of that key. Stick to diatonic chords and everything will sound good together automatically.

Building Diatonic Chords

Here is how diatonic chords are built: take a major scale, and on each degree (each note) of the scale, build a triad by stacking thirds. A triad is three notes: the root, the third, and the fifth.

You only use notes from the scale itself, with no outside notes. Because the scale has both whole steps and half steps in specific places, some of the resulting triads will be major, some will be minor, and one will be diminished. The scale determines the chord quality automatically. 

C Major Scale:   C    D    E    F    G    A    B
Degree:          1    2    3    4    5    6    7

Build triads using only scale notes (every other note):
  Degree 1:  C - E - G   = C major  (I)
  Degree 2:  D - F - A   = D minor  (ii)
  Degree 3:  E - G - B   = E minor  (iii)
  Degree 4:  F - A - C   = F major  (IV)
  Degree 5:  G - B - D   = G major  (V)
  Degree 6:  A - C - E   = A minor  (vi)
  Degree 7:  B - D - F   = B dim    (vii°)

The seven diatonic chords of C major, built by stacking thirds using only notes from the C major scale

The pattern of chord qualities (major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished) is the same in every major key. Learn this pattern once and you can find all seven diatonic chords in any key.

Diatonic chord quality pattern (every major key) I   ii   iii   IV   V   vi   vii° Major Minor Minor Major Major Minor Diminished

The Roman Numeral System

Musicians use Roman numerals to label the chords in a key. This system is powerful because it is key-independent: the same Roman numeral analysis applies in any key. Once you know a progression by its Roman numerals, you instantly know it in every key.

Uppercase (I, IV, V)Major chord on that scale degree
Lowercase (ii, iii, vi)Minor chord on that scale degree
° (vii°)Diminished chord on that scale degree
IThe "tonic": home base, where progressions tend to start and resolve
VThe "dominant": creates the strongest tension, wants to return to I
IVThe "subdominant": moves away from home with a warm, stable feeling
⚠
Case matters. I (uppercase) is a major chord. i (lowercase) would be a minor chord. When analyzing songs and writing chord charts, using the right case communicates chord quality instantly.

The I–IV–V Progression

This is the single most important chord progression in Western music. Blues, country, rock and roll, and folk are built almost entirely on this three-chord framework. Learn it and you can play or understand thousands of songs immediately. 

  Progression:    I       IV      V
  In C major:     C       F       G
  In G major:     G       C       D
  In A major:     A       D       E
  In E major:     E       A       B

The I–IV–V in four common guitar keys, showing the same Roman numerals applied to different root notes

Why does this work so well? The I is home. The IV provides a warm departure. The V creates tension that makes the return to I feel satisfying and resolved. This tension-and-release cycle is the emotional engine of the progression.

✓
Songs that use I–IV–V (or variations): "Johnny B. Goode," "La Bamba," "Twist and Shout," virtually every 12-bar blues ever recorded, and countless others. This progression is not a cliché. It is a proven structure.

The I–V–vi–IV Progression

This is frequently called the "four-chord song" because an enormous number of pop hits use exactly these four chords in this order. If you play these chords in the right key, listeners will immediately recognize the sound from dozens of famous recordings. 

  Progression:    I       V       vi      IV
  In C major:     C       G       Am      F
  In G major:     G       D       Em      C
  In D major:     D       A       Bm      G

The I–V–vi–IV in common guitar keys

Notice that the vi chord (A minor in C major) is the relative minor of the key. Its appearance gives the progression a brief emotional dip, a touch of wistfulness, before the IV brings it back toward resolution. This emotional arc is what makes the progression so universally appealing.

The vi–IV–I–V Progression

This uses the exact same four chords as the I–V–vi–IV, but starts on the vi chord instead of the I. Starting on a minor chord changes the emotional character significantly, making it feel more melancholic and introspective, even though the underlying chords are identical. 

  Progression:    vi      IV      I       V
  In C major:     Am      F       C       G
  In G major:     Em      C       G       D

vi–IV–I–V: same chords as the four-chord song, but with a darker feel from starting on minor

This demonstrates an important principle: specifically which chord you start on shapes the emotional tone just as much as the chord identities themselves. Starting on the vi establishes the minor chord as the emotional home, making the whole progression feel unresolved and searching.

The ii–V–I Progression

If the I–IV–V is the foundation of blues and rock, the ii–V–I is the foundation of jazz. It appears in almost every jazz standard ever written and is considered the most harmonically satisfying three-chord movement in tonal music. 

  Progression:    ii      V       I
  In C major:     Dm      G       C
  In G major:     Am      D       G
  In F major:     Gm      C       F

The ii–V–I: the cornerstone of jazz harmony

The movement works because the ii chord (D minor in C major) sets up tension, the V chord (G major) heightens that tension dramatically, and the I chord (C major) provides the release. It is the most complete tension-and-resolution cycle in Western harmony, which is why jazz composers return to it endlessly.

💡
To hear ii–V–I in action, listen to the first two bars of almost any jazz standard: "Autumn Leaves," "All The Things You Are," "Fly Me to the Moon." The harmonic movement is the same each time, in different keys.

Tension and Resolution: V Wants to Go to I

The most important relationship in all of tonal music is between the V chord (the dominant) and the I chord (the tonic). The V chord contains two notes that create strong pulls toward the I chord: the leading tone (the 7th degree of the scale) wants to rise a half step to the root, and the 4th degree wants to fall a half step to the 3rd.

In C major, G major (the V chord) contains the note B, which is only a half step below C and strongly wants to resolve upward. When G moves to C, that resolution feels satisfying at a nearly physical level. This is not arbitrary. It is a consequence of the acoustic relationship between the notes.

Tonic (I)Home. Stable. Resolved. Tension is released here.
Dominant (V)Maximum tension. Pulls urgently back toward I.
Subdominant (IV)Departure from home. Stable but away from center.
V → IThe most important harmonic movement. Called a "perfect cadence."

Transposing Progressions to Any Key

Here is where the Roman numeral system pays off. Once you know a progression as Roman numerals, you can instantly transpose it to any key. The numerals describe the relationship between chords, not specific chord names.

To transpose: find the diatonic chords of the new key, then apply the same Roman numerals. The process is the same every time. 

  I–IV–V in different keys:

  Key of C:    C    F    G
  Key of G:    G    C    D
  Key of D:    D    G    A
  Key of A:    A    D    E
  Key of E:    E    A    B

  I–V–vi–IV in different keys:

  Key of C:    C    G    Am   F
  Key of G:    G    D    Em   C
  Key of A:    A    E    F#m  D

The same progressions transposed across keys: Roman numerals stay constant while chord names change

✓
A singer asks you to play a song in a different key because it is more comfortable for their voice. If you know the progression as Roman numerals and know your diatonic chords, transposing takes about ten seconds.

Practice

Exercise 1: I–V–vi–IV in G Major

Write out the I–V–vi–IV chord progression in the key of G major. Name each chord. Work it out from the diatonic chords of G major before revealing the answer.

The key of G major:
Diatonic chords of G major are built on the scale G A B C D E F#.
Apply the I–V–vi–IV pattern. What are the four chords?
Answer: G, D, Em, C
  • I = G major (built on G, the 1st degree)
  • V = D major (built on D, the 5th degree)
  • vi = E minor (built on E, the 6th degree, which is minor because of the diatonic pattern)
  • IV = C major (built on C, the 4th degree)

These four open chords (G, D, Em, C) are some of the most common shapes on the guitar. Play them in this order and you will immediately recognize the progression from dozens of songs.

Exercise 2: Diatonic Chords of A Major

The A major scale is: A B C# D E F# G#. Build all seven diatonic chords using the major scale chord quality pattern (Major Minor Minor Major Major Minor Diminished). Name each chord before revealing.

A major scale degrees:
1=A   2=B   3=C#   4=D   5=E   6=F#   7=G#
Apply the diatonic quality pattern to each degree.
The seven diatonic chords of A major:
  • I     = A major
  • ii    = B minor
  • iii   = C# minor
  • IV    = D major
  • V     = E major
  • vi    = F# minor
  • vii° = G# diminished

Notice the pattern is identical in shape to C major, just rooted on A instead of C. The quality pattern (Maj Min Min Maj Maj Min Dim) never changes across major keys.
