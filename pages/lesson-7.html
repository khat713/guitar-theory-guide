MODULE 7
READING MUSIC & SONG ANALYSIS

This is the final module. Everything you have built — the 12 notes, intervals, scales, chords, progressions — comes together here. You will learn to read charts, decode basic sheet music notation, and break down any song you encounter.

Part 1: Reading Chord Charts

A chord chart is the simplest way to notate a song. It contains just the chord names and the structure — how many bars each chord lasts and in what order they appear. No staff notation, no note-by-note detail. Just the harmonic skeleton.

Most working musicians read chord charts daily. Sessions, rehearsals, gigs — the chart gets you through the song quickly without requiring classical reading ability. For guitarists especially, the chord chart is the most practical form of musical notation you will encounter.

💡
A chord chart does not tell you how to play the chords — voicing, strumming pattern, and rhythm are left to the player. It tells you what chords to play and when to change. The interpretation is yours.

The Nashville Number System

The Nashville Number System (NNS) is a shorthand used by session musicians that replaces chord names with numbers. Instead of writing "C F G" you write "1 4 5." The numbers correspond directly to the Roman numerals from Module 6 — just in Arabic numeral form.

The power of the system is that it is completely key-agnostic. A chart written in the NNS works in any key. The band leader calls out the key at the start, and every musician applies the numbers to that key's diatonic chords. No rewriting required when the singer needs a different key.

1 (or I)The tonic chord — home. Major by default.
2 (or ii)The supertonic. Minor by default.
4 (or IV)The subdominant. Major by default.
5 (or V)The dominant. Major by default. Creates the strongest tension.
6 (or vi)The submediant. Minor by default. Often written as 6m.
m (suffix)Appended to indicate minor when a chord deviates from the default quality.
✓
A Nashville chart for a basic country song might look like: 1 1 4 4 / 1 1 5 5 / 4 4 1 1 / 5 5 1 1. That is the complete chord structure — playable in any key within seconds.

Reading a Real Chord Chart: 12-Bar Blues in E

The 12-bar blues is the single most important song form in popular music. It is exactly 12 bars long, repeats indefinitely, and uses the I, IV, and V chords. Here it is in the key of E: 

12-Bar Blues in E

Bar:  | 1   | 2   | 3   | 4   |
      | E   | E   | E   | E   |   (4 bars on the I chord)

Bar:  | 5   | 6   | 7   | 8   |
      | A   | A   | E   | E   |   (2 bars IV, 2 bars I)

Bar:  | 9   | 10  | 11  | 12  |
      | B   | A   | E   | B   |   (V, IV, I, V — the turnaround)

Chords:  E = I (tonic)
         A = IV (subdominant)
         B = V (dominant)

The 12-bar blues in E — each cell represents one full bar. Bar 12 (B = V) creates tension that launches you back to bar 1.

Several things to notice. Each box represents one bar (measure). A chord written once in a bar lasts the entire bar. The last bar (bar 12) ends on the V chord — this is called the turnaround, and its job is to create tension that pulls back to the top of the form. After bar 12 you jump back to bar 1 and repeat.

Bar lines separate the measures. A repeat sign (two dots before a double bar line) tells you to go back to the start. Most chord charts also annotate the number of times a section repeats: "play 4x" means loop the section four times before moving on.

⚠
Chord duration. If a chord name appears once in a bar, it lasts the whole bar. If two chords appear in one bar they split it evenly (two beats each in 4/4). If a chart says "E / / /" the slashes mean "keep playing the same chord for each beat."

Part 2: Basic Sheet Music Concepts

Full sheet music notation is a deep subject, but the foundational concepts are straightforward. You do not need to read music fluently to benefit from understanding the system — even a working knowledge makes learning from scores much faster.

The Staff

Sheet music is written on a staff: five horizontal lines with four spaces between them. Notes are placed either on a line (the line runs through the center of the note head) or in a space (the note head sits between two lines). The higher the note's position on the staff, the higher its pitch. 

  ─────────────────────────────   Line 5  (top)
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 4
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 3  (middle)
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 2
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 1  (bottom)

Notes sit ON lines or IN the spaces between lines.
Higher position = higher pitch.

The five-line staff — the universal grid for notating pitch in Western music

The Treble Clef

Guitar music is written in the treble clef (also called the G clef — it curls around the second line from the bottom, marking it as G). The treble clef defines which note each line and space represents.

Lines (bottom to top)E — G — B — D — F
"Every Good Boy Does Fine"
Spaces (bottom to top)F — A — C — E
"FACE" — spells itself
Middle CWritten on a ledger line just below the staff (one ledger line below line 1)
💡
The mnemonics are worth memorizing: "Every Good Boy Does Fine" for the lines (E G B D F), and "FACE" for the spaces. With those two, you can name any note on the treble clef staff.
⚠
Guitar sounds an octave lower than written. Guitar is a transposing instrument — the notes are notated one octave higher than they actually sound. This keeps guitar music on the treble clef staff without needing constant ledger lines below. When you play a note written as middle C on guitar, it sounds an octave below the middle C a pianist would play.

Note Values and Rhythm

Every note in sheet music has two pieces of information: pitch (which line or space it sits on) and duration (how long it lasts, shown by the shape of the note head and stem).

Whole noteOpen oval, no stem — 4 beats. Lasts an entire bar in 4/4 time.
Half noteOpen oval with a stem — 2 beats. Two fit in one bar of 4/4.
Quarter noteFilled oval with a stem — 1 beat. Four fit in one bar of 4/4.
Eighth noteFilled oval, stem with a flag (or beamed) — half a beat. Eight fit in 4/4.
Sixteenth noteFilled oval, stem with two flags — quarter of a beat. Sixteen fit in 4/4.
Whole (4) = 2 × Half (2) = 4 × Quarter (1) = 8 × Eighth (½) = 16 × Sixteenth (¼) Each level is exactly half the duration of the one above it

Time Signatures

The time signature appears at the beginning of a piece, written as two numbers stacked vertically. The top number tells you how many beats are in each bar. The bottom number tells you what kind of note gets one beat.

4/44 beats per bar, quarter note = 1 beat. The most common time signature in popular music. Also called "common time."
3/43 beats per bar, quarter note = 1 beat. Waltz feel — ONE two three, ONE two three.
6/86 beats per bar, eighth note = 1 beat. Often felt as two groups of three — a compound duple feel.
2/42 beats per bar, quarter note = 1 beat. March feel. Cuts the bar in half compared to 4/4.

For guitar players: if a song feels like you are counting "one two three four" on repeat, it is almost certainly in 4/4. If it feels like a waltz — "one two three" — it is in 3/4. The vast majority of songs you will learn are in 4/4 time.

Part 3: Full Song Analysis

Now we put the full toolkit to work. Analyzing a song means identifying its key, mapping its chord progressions with Roman numerals, and understanding how the song's sections relate to each other harmonically. Here is the process applied step by step to a typical pop song in C major.

Step 1 — Identify the Key

To identify the key of a song, collect all the chord names used in the song and find which major scale they all belong to. Every chord's notes should come from the same parent scale.

Suppose a song uses only these chords: C, G, Am, F, Dm, and Em. Ask: which major scale contains all of these chords as diatonic chords? The answer is C major — all six of these chords appear in the diatonic chord set for C major (I, V, vi, IV, ii, iii). The song is in the key of C major.

💡
Shortcut. The chord the song starts on and the chord it feels most "at rest" on is usually the I chord — the key. If a song begins and ends on C major, and C feels like home throughout, you are almost certainly in C major.

Step 2 — Assign Roman Numerals

Once you know the key, replace every chord name with its Roman numeral from the diatonic chord set of that key. This converts the song from a specific key into a universal pattern. 

Song chords:       C    G    Am   F
Key of C major:    I    V    vi   IV

Song chords:       Dm   G    C
Key of C major:    ii   V    I

Diatonic chords of C major for reference:
  I=C   ii=Dm   iii=Em   IV=F   V=G   vi=Am   vii°=Bdim

Chord names mapped to Roman numerals in the key of C major

With the Roman numerals assigned, you can immediately recognize familiar progressions. C G Am F = I V vi IV — that is the four-chord pop progression from Module 6. Dm G C = ii V I — that is the jazz cadence. The same patterns you learned now appear as recognizable structures inside real songs.

Step 3 — Map the Song Sections

Most pop songs use different chord progressions for different sections. The verse and chorus typically each have their own progression, and the bridge introduces contrast. Mapping each section separately reveals the full harmonic architecture of the song. 

Example: A typical pop song in C major

VERSE:   C  -  G  -  Am  -  F  (repeat)
         I  -  V  -  vi  -  IV

CHORUS:  F  -  C  -  G  -  G  (repeat)
         IV -  I  -  V  -  V

BRIDGE:  Am -  F  -  C  -  G
         vi -  IV -  I  -  V

Verse, chorus, and bridge progressions for a typical pop song in C major

The verse starts on the I chord — it establishes home. The chorus starts on the IV chord — this shifts the harmonic weight, creating a lift that makes the chorus feel bigger and more energetic than the verse. The bridge starts on the vi chord — the relative minor — which creates emotional contrast and a sense of stepping away from the main story before returning for the final chorus.

These are not accidents. Songwriters consciously or intuitively use these harmonic moves because they work on the listener. Understanding them lets you predict what will happen next in a song you have never heard, and replicate them in your own writing.

✓
Chorus lift trick. Starting the chorus on the IV chord instead of the I is one of the most common techniques in pop songwriting. It creates an immediate sense of arrival and uplift. Compare: starting on C (home, expected) vs. starting on F (away from home, surprising but resolved). The F gives the chorus its energy.

Part 4: Applying Theory to Your Playing

Theory is not an academic exercise — it is a toolkit. Every concept in this course has a direct, practical application the moment you pick up your guitar. Here is how the pieces connect to what you actually do when you play.

Use the Key to Know Which Scale to Solo Over

When you identify the key of a song, you immediately know which scale to use for improvising or playing lead. If the song is in G major, the G major scale (and its relative, E minor) contains every note that will sound consonant over the chord progression. You do not need to think note by note — you navigate the scale shape and every note is already in the right family.

This is the single most important practical benefit of learning scales. It transforms soloing from guesswork into informed navigation. You know where you are on the fretboard and why each note you choose sounds the way it does.

💡
A common beginner mistake: learning scale shapes without knowing why. Always connect the shape to its key and its relationship to the song's chords. Then the shape becomes meaningful — not just a fingering pattern but a map of the available musical space.

Use Roman Numerals to Transpose Any Song

Once a song is analyzed as Roman numerals, transposing it to a new key is mechanical. Write down the diatonic chords of the new key, then substitute each Roman numeral. If I–V–vi–IV in C major is C G Am F, then I–V–vi–IV in A major is A E F#m D. Same progression, new key.

This skill is immediately useful. A singer asks you to play lower. A bandmate prefers open-string chords. You need to avoid a capo. In all these cases, you transpose — and if you know the Roman numerals, it takes seconds.

Recognize Common Progressions by Ear

After enough repetition, you will begin hearing I–V–vi–IV or I–IV–V not as chord names but as feelings — patterns your ear recognizes before your brain catches up. This is ear training, and it develops automatically through careful listening combined with the analytical framework you now have.

Practice this: put on any song and try to identify the tonic (the I chord — where does the song feel "at rest"?). Then try to hear when it moves to the IV or V. You do not need perfect pitch for this. You need to know what tension and resolution feel like — and after seven modules, you do.

💡
The V–I move (dominant to tonic) is the most important sound in tonal music. Train your ear to recognize that moment of release when the V chord finally lands on the I. Once you hear it clearly, you will hear it everywhere — in rock, pop, jazz, classical, country, folk. It is the heartbeat of Western harmony.

Understanding Theory Means Understanding Every Song You Hear

This is the real payoff. Music theory is not a set of rules about what you are and are not allowed to play. It is a language for describing what is already happening in the music. When you learn the language, every song you encounter becomes legible — you can read its structure, understand its choices, and borrow its techniques.

You will hear a song on the radio and recognize a ii–V–I. You will listen to a chord change and think "that's a IV to I — plagal cadence — church-y sound." You will watch a guitarist solo and understand why they are reaching for the notes they are reaching for. Theory does not replace feel. It explains feel, and in doing so, gives you more of it.

✓
Every working musician — session players, composers, songwriters, improvisers — uses this framework every day. You have now built it from scratch, one concept at a time. The seven modules of this course contain the foundational vocabulary of all of Western music.
