Part 1: Reading Chord Charts

A chord chart is the easiest way to write down a song. It just shows the chord names and when to change them.

💡
A chord chart doesn't tell you how to play the chords; just which ones and when. The rest is up to you!

The Nashville Number System

The Nashville Number System swaps chord names for numbers. Instead of writing "C F G" you write "1 4 5."

The coolest part: the same chart works in any key. Just say the key and everyone plays the right chords!

1 (or I)Home base. Major chord.
2 (or ii)Minor chord.
4 (or IV)Major chord.
5 (or V)Major chord. Feels tense: wants to go back to 1.
6 (or vi)Minor chord. Often written as 6m.
m (suffix)Add this to show the chord is minor.
✓
A Nashville chart might look like: 1 1 4 4 / 1 1 5 5 / 4 4 1 1 / 5 5 1 1. That's the whole song, playable in any key!

Reading a Real Chord Chart: 12-Bar Blues in E

The 12-bar blues is used in thousands of songs. It's exactly 12 bars long and only uses three chords: I, IV, and V. 

12-Bar Blues in E

Bar:  | 1   | 2   | 3   | 4   |
      | E   | E   | E   | E   |   (4 bars on the I chord)

Bar:  | 5   | 6   | 7   | 8   |
      | A   | A   | E   | E   |   (2 bars IV, 2 bars I)

Bar:  | 9   | 10  | 11  | 12  |
      | B   | A   | E   | B   |   (V, IV, I, V: the turnaround)

Chords:  E = I (tonic)
         A = IV (subdominant)
         B = V (dominant)

The 12-bar blues in E; each cell represents one full bar. Bar 12 (B = V) creates tension that launches you back to bar 1.

Each box is one bar. Bar 12 ends on the V chord (the turnaround), which pulls you back to the start.

⚠
Chord duration. One chord in a bar = play it the whole bar. Two chords = split the bar evenly. Slashes like "E / / /" just mean "keep playing E."

Part 2: Basic Sheet Music

You don't need to read sheet music perfectly to play guitar. But knowing a little helps when you open a song book!

The Staff

Sheet music is written on a staff: five horizontal lines. Notes sit on a line or in a space. Higher up means higher pitch! 

  ─────────────────────────────   Line 5  (top)
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 4
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 3  (middle)
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 2
  ─────────────────────────────   Line 1  (bottom)

Notes sit ON lines or IN the spaces between lines.
Higher position = higher pitch.

The five-line staff: the universal grid for notating pitch in Western music

The Treble Clef

Guitar music uses the treble clef. Use these two tricks to remember all the notes!

Lines (bottom to top)E, G, B, D, F
"Every Good Boy Does Fine"
Spaces (bottom to top)F, A, C, E
"FACE" spells itself
Middle CWritten on a short line just below the staff
💡
Just memorize "Every Good Boy Does Fine" for lines and "FACE" for spaces. That's it!
⚠
Guitar sounds lower than written. Guitar notes sound one octave lower than they look on the page. This is totally normal!

Note Values and Rhythm

Every note tells you what pitch to play and how long to hold it.

Whole noteOpen oval, no stem: 4 beats.
Half noteOpen oval with a stem: 2 beats.
Quarter noteFilled oval with a stem: 1 beat.
Eighth noteFilled oval with a flag: half a beat.
Sixteenth noteFilled oval with two flags: quarter of a beat.
Whole (4) = 2 × Half (2) = 4 × Quarter (1) = 8 × Eighth (½) = 16 × Sixteenth (¼) Each level is exactly half the duration of the one above it

Time Signatures

A time signature shows two numbers at the start of a song. The top number = beats per bar. The bottom number = what kind of note counts as one beat.

4/44 beats per bar. The most common, used in most pop and rock songs.
3/43 beats per bar. Waltz feel: ONE two three, ONE two three.
6/86 beats per bar. Has a bouncy, rolling feel.
2/42 beats per bar. March feel.

Most songs are in 4/4. If you count "one two three four" over and over, you're in 4/4!

Part 3: Analyzing a Song

Analyzing a song means figuring out its key and labeling its chords with numbers. Let's do it step by step!

Step 1: Find the Key

Look at all the chords in the song. Find the major scale they all belong to; that's your key!

Say a song uses C, G, Am, F, Dm, and Em. All those chords live in the C major family. The song is in C major!

💡
Quick trick: The chord the song starts and ends on, the one that feels like "home," is usually the I chord and tells you the key.

Step 2: Label the Chords

Once you know the key, swap each chord name for its Roman numeral. Now the song works in any key! 

Song chords:       C    G    Am   F
Key of C major:    I    V    vi   IV

Song chords:       Dm   G    C
Key of C major:    ii   V    I

Diatonic chords of C major for reference:
  I=C   ii=Dm   iii=Em   IV=F   V=G   vi=Am   vii°=Bdim

Chord names mapped to Roman numerals in the key of C major

See C G Am F? That's I V vi IV, the famous four-chord pop pattern from Module 6. You're already spotting it. Nice work!

Step 3: Map the Song Sections

Most songs have different chords in the verse, chorus, and bridge. Map each section to see how the song is built! 

Example: A typical pop song in C major

VERSE:   C  -  G  -  Am  -  F  (repeat)
         I  -  V  -  vi  -  IV

CHORUS:  F  -  C  -  G  -  G  (repeat)
         IV -  I  -  V  -  V

BRIDGE:  Am -  F  -  C  -  G
         vi -  IV -  I  -  V

Verse, chorus, and bridge progressions for a typical pop song in C major

The chorus starts on the IV chord instead of home. That little trick gives the chorus extra energy!

✓
Chorus lift trick. Starting the chorus on the IV chord is one of the most popular tricks in songwriting. Try it yourself!

Part 4: Using Theory When You Play

Theory helps you every time you pick up your guitar. Here's how!

Know What Scale to Solo Over

When you know the key, you know which scale to use. If the song is in G major, every note in the G major scale will sound great!

💡
Connect every scale shape to its key. Then it becomes a map of where to go, not just a finger exercise.

Move a Song to Any Key

Once you've labeled a song with Roman numerals, changing the key is easy. I–V–vi–IV in C is C G Am F. In A it's A E F#m D. Same pattern, new key!

This is great when a singer needs the song higher or lower.

Train Your Ears

Put on any song and try to find the "home" chord: where does the music feel most settled? That's the I chord!

💡
The V–I move (tension then release) is the most important sound in music. Once you hear it, you'll spot it in every song!

Every Song Makes Sense Now

Music theory is a language for describing what's happening in music. Now that you speak it, every song tells you its secrets!

✓
Every working musician uses this same framework every day. You built it from scratch. That's something to be really proud of!
