MODULE 5
CHORD THEORY

A chord is three or more notes played simultaneously. Once you know how chords are built — which intervals stack on which roots — every chord shape on the guitar becomes something you understand, not just something you memorize.

What Is a Chord?

A chord is three or more notes sounding at the same time. On a guitar, you produce a chord by fretting multiple strings and strumming them together. But the chord isn't the shape your hand makes — it's the set of notes those strings are producing. Understanding that distinction is the key to this entire lesson.

The most fundamental type of chord is the triad — exactly three notes. Every chord you've ever played as a beginner (Em, G, C, Am, D) is a triad or an expanded version of one. Before you can extend chords, you need to understand the three-note core.

💡
Chords aren't magic hand shapes. They are note relationships. Once you learn the formulas, you can build any chord from any root note, anywhere on the neck — no chord diagram required.

Chords Are Stacked Intervals

Every chord is built by starting on a root note and stacking intervals — specific distances measured in semitones — on top of it. The chord formula tells you exactly which intervals to stack. Change the root, keep the formula, and you get the same chord type in a new key.

Triads are always built by stacking two intervals: the distance from root to the second note (the third), and the distance from the second note to the third note (the fifth). The names "third" and "fifth" come from scale degrees — the chord tones land on scale positions 1, 3, and 5.

Triad = Root  +  interval_1  +  interval_2 The two intervals determine the chord quality: major, minor, diminished, or augmented
Root The note the chord is named after. C chord → C is the root. This is home base.
Third The second chord tone, a third above the root. Its quality (major or minor) defines whether the chord sounds bright or dark.
Fifth The third chord tone, a fifth above the root. Usually a perfect fifth — it reinforces the root and gives the chord its stability.

The Major Chord Formula

Major chords sound bright, stable, and resolved — the foundation of countless folk, pop, and rock songs. The formula is two stacked intervals measured in semitones:

Major chord = Root  +  4 semitones  +  3 semitones Root → Major 3rd (+4st) → Perfect 5th (+3st more) — total 7 semitones from root to fifth

The first interval — 4 semitones — is called a major third. The second interval — 3 semitones from the third up to the fifth — is a minor third. Together they land on the perfect fifth, 7 semitones above the root.

The Minor Chord Formula

Minor chords have a darker, more serious emotional quality. The formula is almost identical to major — the only difference is that the first interval is one semitone smaller. That single half-step difference is responsible for the entire shift in mood.

Minor chord = Root  +  3 semitones  +  4 semitones Root → Minor 3rd (+3st) → Perfect 5th (+4st more) — total still 7 semitones from root to fifth

The first interval — 3 semitones — is the minor third. The second interval — 4 semitones from the third up to the fifth — is now a major third. The fifth is still a perfect fifth (7 semitones from root). Only the third changes.

Comparing Major and Minor

The structural difference between a major and minor chord is a single semitone — the third drops by one fret. That tiny shift is responsible for every emotional difference between the two.

Major chord Root + Major 3rd (4st) + Perfect 5th (3st more). Bright, open, stable, happy.
Minor chord Root + Minor 3rd (3st) + Perfect 5th (4st more). Darker, more serious, emotional.
⚠
The most important fact in chord theory: the only difference between a major and minor chord is whether the third is major (4st above root) or minor (3st above root). The fifth is a perfect fifth in both cases. One note, one fret — two completely different emotional worlds.

Chord Qualities: Beyond Major and Minor

Major and minor are the two most common chord qualities, but they are not the only ones. By changing the intervals you stack, you get chords with completely different emotional textures.

Major Root + 4st + 3st. Bright, resolved, happy. The default chord in most pop and folk music.
Minor Root + 3st + 4st. Dark, emotional, serious. The foundation of blues, rock, and ballads.
Diminished Root + 3st + 3st. Tense, unstable, uneasy. Sounds like something unresolved — music in a hurry to move somewhere else.
Augmented Root + 4st + 4st. Dreamy, unstable, floating. Creates a sense of suspension and anticipation.

The Diminished Chord

The diminished triad stacks two minor thirds (3 semitones each). The fifth is now only 6 semitones above the root — a diminished fifth (also called a tritone), which is maximally tense.

Diminished chord = Root  +  3 semitones  +  3 semitones Root → Minor 3rd (+3st) → Diminished 5th (+3st more) — total only 6 semitones from root to fifth

Example: B diminished. Start on B. Add 3 semitones: B → C → C# → D. Add 3 more: D → D# → E → F. Notes: B, D, F. You will encounter this chord naturally as you learn scales and chord progressions — it appears as the seventh chord in every major key.

💡
Diminished chords have a classic "horror movie" sound in isolation — but in context they create powerful tension that resolves beautifully. They appear often in jazz, classical, and dramatic rock passages.

Open Chord Shapes and Their Note Content

Every open chord shape you learned as a beginner is just one of these formulas applied to a root note. The shape is a practical way to hold multiple strings at specific frets — but the reason those frets are chosen is that they produce the correct triad notes.

When you play an open E minor chord (strings 6–1: E, B, E, G, B, E), you are playing the notes E, G, and B — spread across multiple octaves. Three distinct notes repeated across six strings. The open E chord (E, B, E, G#, B, E) gives you E, G#, and B — the E major triad, because G# is 4 semitones above E.

✓
Now that you know the formulas, check any chord you already know: find the root, count up to the third and fifth, and verify it matches the major or minor formula. This exercise permanently connects your muscle memory to your music theory knowledge.

Every Chord Has a Formula

This is the central insight of chord theory: any chord can be built from any root note using the same formula. You are not memorizing a separate chord for every key. You are memorizing a handful of formulas and applying them anywhere.

Want to play a major chord with any note as the root? Apply: root + 4 semitones + 3 semitones. That gives you the three notes to find. On guitar, you then locate those notes across strings to create a playable shape. This is how professional musicians think — not "I know 40 chord shapes," but "I know 4 chord formulas and can execute them anywhere."

Major: R + 4 + 3    Minor: R + 3 + 4    Dim: R + 3 + 3    Aug: R + 4 + 4 All intervals in semitones — apply to any root note to get that chord's three notes

Practice

Exercise 1: Build a D Major Chord

Using the major chord formula (Root + 4 semitones + 3 semitones), build the D major triad from scratch. Count through the chromatic sequence: D – D# – E – F – F# – G – G# – A.

Major formula: Root + 4 semitones + 3 semitones
Root: D

Step 1: Count 4 semitones up from D to find the major third.
Step 2: Count 3 more semitones up from that note to find the perfect fifth.
What are the three notes of D major?
D major — step by step:
  • Root: D
  • +4 semitones: D → D# → E → F → F# — the major third
  • +3 semitones: F# → G → G# → A — the perfect fifth
D major = D, F#, A

When you play an open D chord on guitar, the four strings sounding produce the notes D, A, D, and F# — exactly these three notes (D doubled). The shape produces the formula. The theory and the fretboard are the same thing.

Exercise 2: Build a B Minor Chord

Using the minor chord formula (Root + 3 semitones + 4 semitones), build the B minor triad. Count through: B – C – C# – D – D# – E – F – F#.

Minor formula: Root + 3 semitones + 4 semitones
Root: B

Step 1: Count 3 semitones up from B to find the minor third.
Step 2: Count 4 more semitones up from that note to find the perfect fifth.
What are the three notes of B minor?
B minor — step by step:
  • Root: B
  • +3 semitones: B → C → C# → D — the minor third
  • +4 semitones: D → D# → E → F → F# — the perfect fifth
B minor = B, D, F#

Notice that B, D, and F# all appear in the G major scale (G A B C D E F#). This is not a coincidence — B minor is the relative minor of D major, and both live within the G major key. As you learn more about keys and harmonization in later modules, these connections will come into focus.
