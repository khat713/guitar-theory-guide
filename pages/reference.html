Key Vocabulary

NoteA single musical sound. There are exactly 12 in Western music.
Sharp (#)One fret higher. C# is one fret above C.
Flat (♭)One fret lower. D♭ is one fret below D. Same pitch as C#.
OctaveThe same note, 12 semitones higher or lower. Sounds identical but at a different pitch level.
SemitoneThe smallest interval in Western music. On guitar: 1 fret.
Whole toneTwo semitones. On guitar: 2 frets.
IntervalThe distance between two notes, measured in semitones.
ScaleA specific ordered sequence of notes. Major = bright. Minor = dark.
ChordThree or more notes played simultaneously. Built from stacked intervals.
EnharmonicTwo names for the same pitch: C# and D♭ are enharmonic equivalents.
TabGuitar notation showing fret and string. No music-reading required.

Scale Formulas

Major Scale:   W – W – H – W – W – W – H W = Whole step (2 frets)  |  H = Half step (1 fret)  |  Sounds bright and resolved
Natural Minor:   W – H – W – W – H – W – W Same notes as the relative major, starting 6 steps up  |  Sounds dark and emotional

Chord Formulas

Sounds bright, happy

Root + 3st + 4st

Sounds dark, emotional
Dom. 7th

Tense, wants to resolve

Root + 3st + 3st

Very tense, unstable

Common Chord Progressions

I – IV – V The most fundamental progression in Western music. Blues, rock, country. Sounds complete and resolved.
I – V – vi – IV The "four-chord song." An enormous proportion of modern pop. Catchy, circular, emotionally balanced.
vi – IV – I – V Same four chords starting on vi. Feels more melancholic and introspective than the above.
I – IV – vi – V Common in ballads. The vi adds emotional weight before resolving back through V.
ii – V – I The backbone of jazz. The ii creates tension, V intensifies it, I resolves it. Hear it everywhere.
