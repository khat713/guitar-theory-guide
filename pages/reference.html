Key Vocabulary

Note A single musical sound. There are exactly 12 different notes in Western music.
Sharp (#) One fret higher. C# is one fret above C. Always raises pitch by one semitone.
Flat (♭) One fret lower. D♭ is one fret below D. Same pitch as the sharp below it (D♭ = C#).
Octave The distance from one note to the same note, higher or lower. Exactly 12 semitones = 1 octave.
Semitone The smallest interval in Western music. On guitar = 1 fret.
Whole Tone Two semitones. On guitar = 2 frets.
Interval The distance between two notes, measured in semitones. The building block of scales and chords.
Scale A specific ordered sequence of notes. Major = happy, Minor = sad. Every song is built from a scale.
Chord Three or more notes played simultaneously. Major chords sound bright, minor chords sound dark.
Tab / Tablature Guitar notation showing which fret to press on which string. No music-reading required.
Enharmonic Two different names for the same pitch. C# and D♭ are enharmonic — same fret, different names.

Scale Formulas

Major Scale:   W – W – H – W – W – W – H W = Whole Step (2 frets)  |  H = Half Step (1 fret)  |  Sounds happy, bright
Natural Minor Scale:   W – H – W – W – H – W – W Same notes as the major scale, but starting on a different note  |  Sounds sad, dark

Chord Formulas

Major Chord
Root + 4 semitones + 3 semitones
C Major: C → E (4 up) → G (3 up)
Sounds: bright, happy, resolved
Minor Chord
Root + 3 semitones + 4 semitones
A Minor: A → C (3 up) → E (4 up)
Sounds: dark, sad, emotional
Dominant 7th
Root + 4 + 3 + 3 semitones
G7: G → B → D → F
Sounds: tense, wants to resolve
Diminished
Root + 3 + 3 semitones
B°: B → D → F
Sounds: very tense, unstable

Common Chord Progressions

I – IV – V
The most fundamental progression in Western music. Thousands of blues, rock, and country songs. Sounds complete and resolved.
I – V – vi – IV
The "four-chord song" used in an enormous amount of modern pop. Catchy, circular, and emotionally balanced.
vi – IV – I – V
Same four chords as above but starting on vi. Feels more melancholic and introspective.
I – IV – vi – V
Common in ballads. The vi gives it emotional weight before resolving back through V.
ii – V – I
The backbone of jazz harmony. The ii chord creates tension that V intensifies, then I resolves it perfectly.
